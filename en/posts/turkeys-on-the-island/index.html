The Turkeys on the Island

Posted on 2026-10-25

The turkeys on the island divided everything in the world into three categories:

What had already happened could be measured. What had yet to happen could be predicted. What would never happen need not be discussed.

In year 327 of the Scientific Era, Hertz, president of the Turkey Academy of Sciences, proposed this classification. It was carved into a slab of black basalt. Beneath it were two lines that countless young turkeys copied into their notebooks:

When a thing cometh into being, it is subject to law.

When the fulness of time is come, all things subject to law can be foretold.

For a very long time, no one seriously questioned these words. After all, they really were getting better and better at predicting the world.

Turkey Island was small. A walk from the White Cliffs in the east to the salt marsh in the west took only a morning. An extinct volcano stood at its center, with berry bushes, moss, and wild wheat growing at its foot. To the north lay a forest of low pines, where foxes occasionally appeared. The southern shore faced a warm current and rarely froze, even in winter.

The earliest turkeys knew nothing of science. They knew only to shelter beneath trees when it rained, run when they saw a fox, and peck about for food when they were hungry. Spring came, but they did not know why. Some years there were plenty of berries; other years there were few. Sometimes the fox population suddenly grew, and half the young birds failed to survive. Sometimes a storm swept through, and an entire beach disappeared.

The world was like an invisible hand, sometimes stroking them, sometimes pushing them into the mud for no reason at all.

The first turkey who tried to understand that hand was called Al.

Al had no instruments and did not know what a calendar was. He had simply noticed that each year in late spring, after the moon first became a slender silver hook pointing a certain way, three more cycles of waxing and waning would pass before the blueberries on the northern slope began to turn blue.

The moon did not ripen the blueberries, and Al knew nothing of day length or plant growth cycles. He had merely happened upon a fairly stable correspondence between two recurring phenomena. The moon became his clock.

After several years of observation, he made an announcement for the first time while the berries were still unripe: “In three days, the first ripe blueberry will appear on the northern slope.”

The other turkeys laughed.

“Blueberries ripen when they ripen,” they said. “What good is counting moons?”

Al did not argue. He went to the northern slope and kept watch beside the bushes. On the afternoon of the third day, the first blueberry did indeed turn blue.

For the first time in the island’s recorded history, a turkey had said when something would happen before it happened.

The turkeys designated that year as Year One of the Scientific Era.

Science developed slowly at first.

Turkeys had no hands, which made building instruments especially difficult. They moved stones with their beaks, scratched lines on clay tablets with their claws, and planted thin twigs in the ground to record the length of their shadows. Gradually, they discovered that shadows changed with the seasons, that the rise and fall of the tides were related to the moon, and that when a certain red beetle emerged from the soil in large numbers, rain often followed within three days.

The foxes’ appearances also followed patterns. They generally patrolled their territories along fixed routes and preferred to hunt at dusk. When hares grew scarce on the northern slope, attacks on turkeys increased.

And so the first observation tower was built. It was followed by a meteorological observatory, a Bureau of Food Statistics, an Institute for Fox Behavior Research, an Institute of Astronomy, a Department of Disease Control, and an Island Committee.

From then on, every turkey received a number at birth, the area of every berry patch had to be registered, and the beginning and end of every rainfall had to be recorded. Where a fox appeared, which way it was heading, how fast it was moving, even how long it stopped to sniff beside a particular rock—all of it became data that had to be preserved.

More than a century passed, and the caves of the extinct volcano filled with clay tablets. Later, the turkeys learned to fire pottery shards, then discovered that the dried leaves of a certain broad-leaved plant made a better writing surface.

Little by little, the world changed from a frightening chaos into an enormous table of data.

In year 182 of the Scientific Era, the Turkey Academy of Sciences introduced the concept of “prediction accuracy.” They collected every officially issued prediction on the island and compared each one with what had actually happened.

In the first year, the accuracy rate was just one percent.

Many turkeys were disheartened, but the president at the time declared in the square:

“One percent is not failure. One percent means that, for the first time, the future has opened a crack to let us in!”

After that, everything began to accelerate.

One percent. Three percent. Seven percent. Twelve percent.

The models grew increasingly complex. They no longer merely predicted whether it would rain tomorrow; they could say roughly what time the rain would begin. They no longer merely judged whether the year’s harvest would be plentiful; they could estimate the yield of each berry patch to within three baskets.

The foxes, too, lost their mystery.

The institute gave every fox in the northern forest a name: Red Ear, Limp, Gray Tail, Short Fang. They knew that Red Ear patrolled the western slope roughly once every four days, that Gray Tail hunted seventeen percent less often on nights with a full moon, and that Limp rarely crossed the stream when the temperature fell below five degrees.

Deaths from fox attacks declined year by year.

In time, the Turkey Academy of Sciences even began publishing an Annual Death List in advance.

May 14. No. 7021. Juvenile. Death by falling from a cliff.

June 2. No. 388. Elderly male. Death from lung disease.

July 19. No. 9412. Killed by Gray Tail. Location: northern slope.

At first, the turkeys could not accept the list. Those whose numbers appeared on it wept and protested. Some tried to flee the places where they were predicted to die.

No. 9412 was one of them. The model predicted that Gray Tail would kill him at 12:13 p.m. on July 19, so on the night of July 18, he fled into the southern salt marsh. But early the next morning, driven by hunger and fear, he returned to the northern slope. At 12:17 p.m., Gray Tail sprang from behind the bushes.

The time of his death differed from the prediction by only four minutes.

After that, open defiance of predictions became increasingly rare.

The turkeys gradually accepted an idea: free will did not break the laws that governed the world.

Free will was merely the part of those laws that had yet to be fully understood.

In year 290 of the Scientific Era, prediction accuracy passed eighty percent, and society as a whole began to change.

Turkey farmers no longer relied on experience to decide when to sow, because the agricultural department would tell them the best day. Young turkeys seldom chose mates entirely by feeling, either; the Island Committee calculated which pairings were most likely to produce healthy offspring. Before a journey, turkeys checked the probability of an accident. When they argued, they first consulted the model of turkey social behavior to see who would apologize first.

Art, too, gradually became something that could be predicted.

Before a new play opened, the theater would receive an audience response forecast from the turkeys’ cultural affairs department: the first laughter in the seventh minute, applause in the twenty-third, approximately sixty-two percent of the audience in tears in the fifty-eighth, a curtain call lasting four minutes and eleven seconds.

Few turkeys saw anything wrong with this. They had gained a sense of security their ancestors had never known.

Not knowing the future had once been part of life. Now it was an almost unimaginable state of backwardness.

At school, children read ancient stories. When they learned that their ancestors had frozen to death in a sudden downpour, they would ask their teachers in astonishment, “Why didn’t they know beforehand?”

“Because science wasn’t advanced enough back then,” the teachers would patiently explain.

The children clucked in sympathy.

But one unwelcome thing continued to trouble the Turkey Academy of Sciences: error.

Even an accuracy rate of 99.9 percent meant that one prediction in every thousand was still wrong. The scientists called these errors “residual ignorance” and found them increasingly intolerable. And so the island embarked on the greatest scientific undertaking in its history: the Omniscience Project.

Tens of thousands of observation stations spread across the island. Every tree had a number. Every stream had a record of its flow. Wind speed, humidity, clouds, lightning, tides, disease, insects, berries, foxes, bird migration, soil temperature, changes in the magnetic field—everything that could be measured was fed into a single model.

Then they began recording their own thoughts.

Every evening, all residents had to fill out a psychological survey: How afraid had they been today? How hungry? Had they thought of migrating? Had they doubted science?

The last question caused some controversy. The Turkey Academy of Sciences offered a simple explanation: if “doubting science” could itself affect behavior, then doubt was of course a variable, and variables ought to be observed.

The dissenters thought it over and could find no immediate objection. So they filled out the forms as well.

In year 399 of the Scientific Era, prediction accuracy reached 99.99 percent.

All of Turkey Island was swept up in a kind of religious fervor. More and more turkeys wore circular badges on their chests, engraved with the first line from the basalt monument:

When a thing cometh into being, it is subject to law.

Only one final sliver of error remained between them and perfection.

That was the year Nakabachi joined the Turkey Academy of Sciences. He was assigned to an obscure department that studied anomalous phenomena at sea. It was one of the dullest fields of research on the island. The ocean stretched without end, yet had little to do with life on the island. The tides could already be predicted precisely, and there were well-established models for storms. Now and then, a branch, a dead fish, or a seed from some unknown place drifted across the water. Apart from that, there was nothing.

On New Year’s Day in year 400 of the Scientific Era, Nakabachi began his watch at the White Cliffs. Regulations required him to observe the horizon five times a day: at six and nine in the morning, at noon, at three in the afternoon, and at dusk.

Turkey Island used a very simple calendar: an ordinary year had 365 days, with a leap day added at the end of winter every four years. Thus, the eleven full years from New Year’s Day in year 400 to the end of year 410 included three leap years—400, 404, and 408—for a total of 4,018 days.

Nakabachi completed 20,090 scheduled observations. Of the resulting records, 20,089 contained the same words:

No abnormal findings.

The only exception came early one morning in year 406. Far out at sea, he saw an unfamiliar dark shape floating on the water. It lingered for a few minutes, then vanished into the fog.

Nakabachi submitted a report. The model offered several explanations: a large piece of driftwood, forty-two percent probability; a whale, thirty-one percent; a visual anomaly caused by low clouds, 26.9 percent; unknown, 0.1 percent.

The report was then filed away.

The next day, everything at sea was as usual. Nothing similar happened in the years that followed. In year 411, automated equipment for observing the sea took over most of the watch at the White Cliffs, and Nakabachi no longer needed to climb up five times a day.

But a habit of eleven years was not so easily broken. Sometimes, when he passed that way, he still brought his telescope and took a look out to sea.

In October of year 412 of the Scientific Era, the miracle finally happened.

The Omniscience Model ran for seven days and seven nights. On the morning of the eighth day, the doors of the Turkey Academy of Sciences opened, and its president, Sauron, stepped out into the square. His feathers were almost entirely white. Tens of thousands of turkeys crowded around the foot of the volcano, waiting for him to announce the results.

“Over the past year, we issued a total of four million, eight hundred and seventy-two thousand, one hundred and ninety-four predictions.”

The square was so quiet that the wind could be heard brushing through feathers.

Sauron glanced down at the paper.

“Of those, the number that proved correct was—four million, eight hundred and seventy-two thousand, one hundred and ninety-four.”

For a moment, no one cheered.

The number was so complete that they needed a moment to understand what it meant. It seemed as though some shortfall ought to follow.

At last, Sauron raised his head.

“An accuracy rate of one hundred percent.”

A few seconds later, an immense cry erupted across the island.

The turkeys beat their wings and bumped their chests together. Elderly turkeys lay on the ground and wept. The bell of the Turkey Academy of Sciences rang a hundred times in succession. More than four centuries of scientific history seemed, at that moment, to have reached their destination.

They had fulfilled their ancestors’ dream.

The future had not yet happened, but it could already be leafed through, just like the past.

That evening, the Turkey Academy of Sciences issued its first Complete Future Report, listing, one by one, the events of the following day:

4:13 a.m. Rain on the northern slope.

6:07 a.m. A thirteenth-generation descendant of Red Ear catches a rabbit beside the stream.

7:20 a.m. Residents of the eastern district begin to eat.

8:00 a.m. School begins.

10:35 a.m. Juvenile No. 89757 falls in the playground and scrapes his right knee.

12:00 noon. Temperature: 21.4 degrees.

No major events in the afternoon.

Sunset: 5:43 p.m.

At the end of the report were the words:

Total turkey deaths across the island tomorrow: 3.

One from disease, one from a fox attack, one from old age.

It was the most ordinary of days. Before it had even begun, it was already known in full.

That night, the turkeys slept with an uncommon peace of mind.

At 4:13 the next morning, rain began on the northern slope, exactly on time.

At 6:07, a fox caught a hare beside the stream. At 7:20, residents of the eastern district began to eat. Everything happened as the report had said it would.

In the hall of the Turkey Academy of Sciences, a row of figures glowed on an enormous scoreboard:

Current accuracy: 100.000000%

At nine that morning, Nakabachi happened to pass the White Cliffs.

Under the regulations, he had long since ceased to be required to stop there. The Omniscience Model’s forecast for the sea that day read: normal at nine, normal at noon, normal at three. No abnormal findings all day.

But he raised the telescope he carried with him anyway.

At 9:03, he saw several black dots appear on the horizon.

The dots slowly grew larger.

At 9:11, Nakabachi adjusted his telescope to its highest magnification.

He saw things he had never seen before.

Several enormous white bodies floated on the water, with straight poles rising from them and broad sheets of cloth bellied out by the wind. These things were heading toward Turkey Island.

Nakabachi stood there for a long time without lowering the telescope.

His heart beat faster and faster. Something the model said did not exist was drawing steadily closer.

He did not know which box on the psychological survey this feeling belonged in. Later, he would never have another chance to fill out that form.

At 9:14, he turned and ran toward the Turkey Academy of Sciences.

“There’s something out at sea.”

The duty officer glanced up at him, then down at the prediction sheet.

“No, there isn’t.”

“I saw it.”

“The model says there’s nothing there.”

“But I really did see it.”

Looking a little exasperated, the duty officer pulled up another sheet. “The forecast of your mental state today shows that at 9:12, you will experience a visual misperception lasting approximately eleven minutes.”

Nakabachi hesitated.

“The model predicted that I would come here to report it?”

“Of course.”

“Then what did it predict I would see?”

“Refraction through clouds.”

“They aren’t clouds.”

The duty officer looked at the screen. “You will believe they aren’t clouds. That’s in the prediction too.”

Suddenly, Nakabachi did not know what to say. Even the words he had not yet spoken seemed already to be written on that sheet.

A system that incorporated the arguments against itself seemed impossible to refute from within.

He turned and rushed out of the Turkey Academy of Sciences.

According to the model, he should have begun to calm down at 9:26, then returned to his office. But he did not. This time, the Omniscience Model was wrong. No turkey noticed. Ever since accuracy had reached one hundred percent, no one had thought the figure needed checking anymore.

At 10:02, the first ship reached shore.

Six humans jumped down from the ship, wearing heavy boots and carrying objects the turkeys had never seen. One crouched to examine the soil. Another looked up and saw turkeys foraging in the distance.

“God,” he said. “The place is full of wild turkeys.”

His companions laughed.

“Well, that’s dinner sorted.”

At 10:07, the first gunshot rang out.

A turkey in the eastern district fell.

There was no entry for this in the Omniscience Model. According to the model, that turkey was supposed to die of liver disease seven years later.

Then came a second shot, a third, a fourth.

At first, the turkeys did not even run.

They raised their heads and stared blankly toward the sound. Gunfire belonged to no known category of danger. There was no scent of foxes in the air, no tremor in the ground. The sky was clear. Air pressure and temperature were normal. Every danger indicator read zero.

So they stood where they were.

Until more and more of their companions fell.

At last, panic spread. Residents poured toward the Turkey Academy of Sciences, desperate to know what had happened, how much longer it would last, and where they should flee.

The turkey scientists frantically ran their calculations again:

Instruments normal.

Inputs normal.

Parameters normal.

Algorithm normal.

The model still returned:

Deaths today: 3.

By then, more than four hundred turkeys were dead.

President Sauron stood in the computer room, checking the data over and over.

The Omniscience Model contained more than four hundred years of Turkey Island’s history: every rainfall, every fox attack, every disease, every migration, every turkey’s birth and death. Their records were so comprehensive that they had always believed complete data would eventually amount to a complete world.

Nakabachi burst through the computer room door.

“Our data…” He caught his breath. “There’s nothing in it from the other side of the sea.”

Sauron raised his head and looked at Nakabachi without speaking.

“We study waves, tides, things that drift ashore, and storms. We classify every kind of seed that washes onto the beach.” Nakabachi was panting. “But we’ve never been to the other side of the sea. Not a single turkey knows what’s there.”

More gunshots sounded outside.

“All this time,” Nakabachi said, “we thought the island was the world.”

Sauron slowly turned toward the screen.

100.000000%.

The number was still glowing there: perfect, solemn, unassailable.

The model was still computing the world enclosed by more than four hundred years of data, an eternal island of data. There, everything had a number, and every future event belonged to an established category. Tomorrow might be different, but it could only ever be a rearrangement of yesterday.

At noon, the temperature was 21.4 degrees.

That prediction was still entirely correct.

The humans lit fires on the beach and piled up the turkeys they had killed. Some were loaded into the ships’ holds; others were butchered on the spot. After three in the afternoon, the gunshots grew less frequent, and the surviving turkeys fled into the northern forest.

They knew only how to hide from foxes. Foxes patrolled fixed territories. Foxes stopped hunting when they had eaten their fill. Humans did not.

Foxes did not set fire to bushes. Humans did.

Around five that evening, Sauron and Nakabachi hid in a volcanic cave that had once held ancient clay tablets. Now and then, they heard boots crunching over gravel outside.

Sauron looked at a clay tablet before him. It was a replica of one from Year One of the Scientific Era, inscribed with Al’s earliest blueberry records:

A thin crescent.

A full moon.

A waning moon.

A thin crescent again.

After three cycles, the blueberries ripen.

The same the second year.

Still the same the third.

More than four hundred years of scientific history had begun with these few lines.

After a long time, Sauron asked:

“Nakabachi, do you think Al was wrong?”

Nakabachi shook his head.

“No.”

“The patterns in the blueberries, the patterns in the foxes—were they real?”

“They were.”

“And the tides, the seasons, the diseases?”

“All real.”

The footsteps outside the cave drew closer.

“Then where, exactly, did we go wrong?”

Sauron sighed and said no more.

At 5:43 that evening, the sun sank below the horizon, exactly on time.

It was the last prediction of the Omniscience Model to be verified as correct that day.

At 5:44, the humans discovered the cave.

The gunfire lasted less than a minute.

Then the island grew quiet again.

The next morning, no turkey went to the meteorological observatory to record the rain. No turkey numbered the foxes. No turkey filled out a psychological survey. The tides rose and fell as usual, and the foxes patrolled their territories along the old paths. The laws that had been discovered were still at work. There was simply no one left to check the Omniscience Model’s predictions.

Three days later, the humans left.

They took what they could carry. The remaining turkeys were eaten by foxes and seabirds. A month later, weeds pushed through the cracks between the bricks in the Academy’s square. A year later, a winter storm brought down the observation tower at the White Cliffs.

Twenty-three years later, in the damp volcanic caves, the paper holding more than four hundred years of knowledge had rotted away.

Only the basalt monument still stood at the foot of the volcano.

On it were the words:

When a thing cometh into being, it is subject to law.

When the fulness of time is come, all things subject to law can be foretold.

Later, a fig tree took root beside the monument. Year by year, its roots swelled, and cracks appeared in the stone.

Many more years passed, and the stone split down the middle.

The first line tumbled into the mud. The second still faced the sky. There was no turkey left on the island who could read it.

The sea rose and receded, again and again. Storms, earthquakes, and tides slowly redrew the island’s outline.

The northern pines disappeared, and new forests grew from the humus. The White Cliffs collapsed. The salt marsh sank beneath the sea. The cave that had held the clay tablets caved in deep within the mountain. The bricks and stones of the Turkey Academy of Sciences were forced apart by roots, then buried under layer upon layer of silt.

Generations of foxes came and went. The calls of the birds changed countless times. The strait widened, then narrowed again in some cold age. The fig tree that had split the stone fell and rotted, becoming dark soil beneath the roots of another tree. Later still, the trees were gone as well.

Far away, the shapes of continents changed. Precession slowly shifted the stars. The moon the turkeys had once used to reckon the seasons still rose night after night, but no living thing knew anymore that it had once been the clock of a turkey named Al.

Rain wore the stone’s broken edges smooth. The inscriptions grew so faint that they resembled natural markings. Knowledge, fear, arguments, and an accuracy rate of one hundred percent all sank together into the strata. Unless someone went looking for them, it was as though they had never existed.

After an unknown number of years, visitors from another planet came here and discovered the remains of the turkey civilization. They set up a stone monument once more and added a third line:

When their appointed hour is come, all things that can be foretold shall pass away.
