Data Risks and Illegal Firearms

Posted on 2026-09-23

This article contains AI-generated content and may contain factual errors, omissions, misinterpretations, or outdated information. For information that may affect important decisions, please verify it against official materials, primary sources, or other reliable sources.

Q: In the digital world, each person’s existence is data. If other people or AI agents can store, read, and change that data at will, what problems might arise as AI becomes more capable? What similarities does it have with illegal possession of firearms?

A: If we understand a person’s digital “existence” as a constantly updated collection of data—identity, relationships, assets, behavioral history, preferences, permissions, reputation, location, health and employment records, and even a “personality model” that AI builds from all of this—then the central question goes beyond “privacy breaches.” It becomes: who has the power to alter this digital entity, and can those changes affect the person in the physical world?

As AI agents become more capable, the danger may progress from “someone knows things about you” to “someone can use what they know to act in your name.”

The problems arise at several levels:

  • Reading enables prediction. With enough data, AI can infer a person’s vulnerabilities, habits, social network, spending power, mental state, and likely next moves. That enables highly personalized manipulation, fraud, persuasion, or discrimination.
  • Storage makes information persistent. People forget; databases do not. A single act from ten years ago might be reinterpreted and combined by a more capable future model, producing consequences no one could have foreseen at the time.
  • Modification can rewrite reality. If credit, education, medical and employment records, account permissions, and even social relationships depend on databases, changing data is more than editing words that describe you. It may change whether you can board a plane, get a loan, enter your workplace, use your assets, or even prove who you are.
  • Agency enables impersonation. When AI can send emails, transfer money, sign contracts, file applications, and communicate with other agents, whoever acquires the relevant data and credentials may gain control of a kind of “digital double.”
  • Combining data creates a leap in capability. Data that seems harmless in isolation can be dangerous in combination. A name, schedule, voice sample, contact list, purchase history, and a few photographs can give AI an attack capability far greater than any one database provides.
  • Scale creates an imbalance of power. In the past, a malicious person could target only a few people at once. An AI agent can automatically search, analyze, probe, deceive, and maintain interactions with hundreds of thousands or even millions of people.

The concept we may need to worry about most, then, is not a “data leak” in the traditional sense, but unauthorized control over a person’s digital identity and agency.

This is where the comparison with possessing an illegal firearm becomes interesting.

Firearms are tightly regulated not only because firing one can cause harm, but because possession itself confers the ability to impose force unilaterally. In an orderly society, that ability is constrained by identity checks, licensing, accountability, and limits on use.

Broad access to detailed personal data, together with AI agents able to act on that data, may create a similar kind of risk based on capability.

The deepest similarity is not that “data is like ammunition.” It is this:

An unauthorized person has acquired a power they should not have—one that can have serious, hard-to-reverse effects on others.

An illegal firearm provides the capacity for physical coercion. Unauthorized control of data and AI agents may provide the capacity for digital coercion.

Owning a gun, for example, does not mean someone will commit a crime. Society still asks who owns it, how they got it, whether it is registered, and who can use it. Likewise, an AI system holding detailed records on 100 million people will not necessarily abuse them. But we should ask: Why does it have that data? Who authorized it? What can it do? Who can invoke it? Can its actions be audited? Can its permissions be revoked?

Both also present a problem of capability spreading. An illegal gun that enters an underground market can change hands repeatedly. Once sensitive data is copied, it is often even harder to recover: copying costs almost nothing, and the original holder may never know it happened.

This analogy has an important limit, however.

Data and firearms are not equivalent.

One of a gun’s primary purposes is to exert physical force. Data, by contrast, has enormous legitimate value: medicine, research, finance, education, personalized services, and AI assistants all depend on it. Future data governance therefore cannot simply follow a “ban possession” model. It is closer to managing nuclear material, bank access, or root privileges in cloud infrastructure: the point is to govern permissions, purpose, scope, and responsibility.

In fact, I think a more precise analogy might be:

Personal data in the AI age ≈ a mixture of a weapon, an identity card, a bank key, and authority to act on someone’s behalf.

Data alone may not cause harm. Coupled with an AI that can carry out actions, however, it becomes something qualitatively different.

The traditional internet era looked roughly like this:

Data → Information

The era of AI agents is moving toward:

Data → Understanding → Decision → Action

Once the chain reaches that final step, data security becomes more than a question of privacy. It becomes a broader question of agency and autonomy.

We may therefore need an idea akin to “licensing digital weapons.” What it would govern is not any individual dataset, but the combination of data × model × permissions × ability to act.

An AI that can view your calendar poses limited risk. Let it see your calendar and email, and the risk grows. Let it read your email and bank account, and the risk grows further. If it can also send messages, transfer funds, and sign documents for you, it has acquired a degree of digital agency.

At that point, the key question for a security system is no longer only:

“What can this AI see?”

It is also:

“What can this AI cause to happen?”

I think this is a crucial change in perspective for understanding future AI safety: from protecting data to protecting people’s digital autonomy.

Q: If countries come into conflict, what might happen in the digital world?

A: Continuing from the premise that a person = data + permissions + digital agency, the digital world may become more than a supporting theater for physical war. It may gradually become an independent space in which a state must be able to survive.

The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) already treats cyber operations that disrupt electricity, healthcare, water, telecommunications, transport, government, and financial systems as real humanitarian risks. Its work in 2026 also identifies AI in conflict-related ICT operations as an emerging risk. (ICRC)

The first move may be an ambush, not an attack

In a conventional war, an army cannot secretly park tanks inside another country’s power plant during peacetime.

The digital world is different.

Long before a conflict begins, a state may already be trying to enter another state’s communications systems, cloud platforms, supply chain software, government networks, or critical infrastructure. It may break nothing at first. It may simply look for permissions, map systems, and establish persistent access.

Think of this as:

Prepositioning forces in the digital world.

That access might be activated only when a crisis begins. Government agencies have publicly discussed this risk of “prepositioned access” to critical infrastructure.

There is also a dangerous uncertainty here: when defenders discover an unfamiliar program in a system, they cannot immediately tell whether it is gathering intelligence or preparing for more serious damage. The ICRC has noted that this uncertainty itself can increase the risk of miscalculation and escalation. (International Review)

A deeper possibility: attacking a state’s data reality

As a modern state becomes more dependent on digital systems, it effectively exists in two spaces at once:

Physical state = population + land + buildings + armed forces + factories + roads

Digital state = identity databases + bank ledgers + land registries + tax records + medical records + business data + communications networks + government systems + AI systems

The targets of an attack could therefore change.

In past wars, attackers destroyed:

Bridges, railways, power plants, warehouses.

A future digital conflict might instead seek to make:

Banks unable to tell who owns how much money; Governments unable to verify who is who; Logistics systems unable to locate goods; Hospitals unable to read patient records; Businesses unable to tell which orders are real; Armed forces unable to tell which commands are genuine.

This is fundamentally different from an ordinary “hack.”

The most severe attack may not delete data at all. It may make data untrustworthy.

If a database is deleted, you know it is broken.

But if only 0.1% of its records are precisely altered, the problem may be harder:

You do not know which records are true.

A peculiar weapon emerges: contamination of reality

Imagine a country with a digital identity system covering 50 million people.

An attacker would not need to erase every identity.

Suppose it quietly changed:

  • 0.5% of dates of birth;
  • 0.2% of bank account mappings;
  • Some land ownership records;
  • Some medical records;
  • Some government staff permissions.

The technical systems might still appear to run normally.

But society would face a grave problem:

Reality in the databases would no longer match reality in the physical world.

The state would have to answer:

“Which version is real?”

Paper archives?

The database?

Blockchain records?

Documents kept by individuals?

Bank backups?

Government backups?

This is no longer just a cybersecurity problem. It approaches a problem of:

Reality Integrity.

AI could transform this kind of conflict further

In the past, attacking a country’s digital systems required large numbers of people.

AI agents could automate many steps.

In theory, they could analyze large numbers of networks, accounts, personal relationships, software vulnerabilities, and behavioral patterns at once, then identify the most valuable paths for an attack.

The attack model might shift from:

Hacker → System

to:

AI → Data → Person → AI → System

The most dangerous move at that point might not even be attacking software.

It might be attacking the other side’s AI.

A country might one day have AI systems for public finance, the military, logistics, healthcare, diplomacy, energy dispatch, and city management.

If those systems depend on databases and models, attackers may not need to destroy them.

They only need to change the world those systems see.

In other words:

Data poisoning → Model perception → Decision → Action

Data is poisoned ↓ AI forms a false view ↓ AI makes a wrong decision ↓ Physical systems carry it out

That creates a phenomenon rarely seen in past wars:

Making the adversary’s systems attack themselves.

Information warfare would change too

The core of earlier propaganda was:

Make people believe false information.

In the AI era, it could become:

Make the entire information environment unable to prove what is true.

That is far more serious than creating a single fake news story.

If video, audio, and documents can be generated, identities can be simulated, and social accounts can be operated automatically, society’s greatest problem may not be:

“There is too much false information.”

It may be:

The cost of verifying authenticity keeps rising.

If that cost exceeds what society can bear, systems of trust will come under pressure.

Recent ICRC research also identifies misinformation, hate speech, and the way digital technologies amplify conflict as major risks to civilians. It notes that digitalization is blurring the boundary between civilian and military digital infrastructure. (ICRC)

A frequently overlooked issue: private companies become part of the structure of war

Much of today’s digital infrastructure is not state-owned.

Cloud computing, satellite communications, operating systems, chips, AI models, social networks, DNS, CDNs, undersea cables, and cybersecurity services are often controlled by companies.

Future conflicts between states may therefore take a form that was once very rare:

State ↔ Company ↔ State

rather than simply:

State ↔ State.

The decisions of a cloud provider, AI company, satellite communications firm, or chipmaker could affect a country’s digital capabilities.

In its work from 2025 to 2026, the ICRC has specifically discussed how the involvement of technology companies, civilian hackers, and AI in conflicts complicates the line between civilian and military activity. (ICRC)

The greatest danger may be digital conflict spilling over

A bomb in the physical world usually has a geographic range.

Malware may not.

A tool designed to attack a system in Country A could spread through interconnected networks, supply chains, or misconfiguration to:

Countries B and C, hospitals, logistics companies, or ordinary people’s computers.

The ICRC points out that cyber tools carry a distinctive risk of proliferation: once used, a tool can be reused by other actors, and highly connected networks mean that an operation against one system may affect others. (International Review)

The digital era may thus produce a strange geography of war:

Only two countries are fighting, but the battlefield could span the globe.

Even “national borders” would need to be redefined

Traditional borders are easy to understand:

A country’s location is marked by coordinates on a map.

But where are the borders of a digital state?

The questions might become:

Which data belongs to this country?

Which AI systems can act on its behalf?

Which servers count as critical infrastructure?

Which digital identities does the state protect?

Does attacking a private cloud company’s servers amount to attacking a country?

These questions have not all been resolved.

The United Nations has developed a framework for responsible state behavior in the use of ICTs, covering international law, norms of behavior, confidence-building measures, and capacity building. Its discussions also include norms against intentionally damaging critical infrastructure that provides services to the public. A global mechanism launched in 2026 continues to discuss these issues. (UN Official Document System)

In an actual armed conflict, the ICRC’s position is that existing international humanitarian law also applies to cyber operations. That includes distinguishing military from civilian targets, proportionality, and precautions in attack; facilities such as hospitals receive special protection. (International Review)

This brings us back to the earlier analogy of the “illegal firearm.”

Strategic capability in a conventional war might be written as:

Weapons × Armed forces × Logistics

The AI era may gradually add another combination:

Data × Models × Permissions × Computing power × Networks × Ability to act

Any one of these elements may be harmless on its own.

Combined, however, they could produce a capability approaching that of a “strategic weapon.”

That is why I think the concept most worth discussing in the future may go beyond:

Cybersecurity

to:

Digital sovereignty

It includes at least three things:

No one else can arbitrarily change my data.

No one else can control the AI that acts on my behalf.

Databases that determine social reality must be able to demonstrate their integrity.

Take the thought one step further, and a fascinating, difficult question emerges:

If a country has not been occupied, no buildings have been bombed, and no soldiers have crossed its border—but another country controls its identity system, financial system, government AI, communications, and social systems of trust—has it, in effect, already “lost” the war?

I think this may be one of the most important questions to study about war, sovereignty, and the definition of a state in the AI era.
