Dinosaurs and Birds

Posted on 2026-10-11

Q: What exactly happened during the extinction of the dinosaurs? As dinosaurs evolved into birds, how numerous were dinosaurs and birds, and how were they faring while they coexisted? In the natural environment of that time, what survival advantages did birds have over dinosaurs?

A: What went extinct about 66 million years ago was all the “non-avian dinosaurs”; birds themselves are a branch of dinosaurs, and many birds also went extinct at the time. Today’s birds descend from some of the lineages that survived. Explaining this history therefore requires answering two questions: how the impact destroyed ecosystems, and why some birds managed to survive. Wiley Online Library

First, consider the extinction process. Scientists can now reconstruct its main stages, but cannot say exactly how many years after the impact the last dinosaur died.

Approximate stage What happened Effects on living organisms
At the moment of impact An asteroid roughly 10 kilometers in diameter struck near what is now Mexico’s Yucatán Peninsula, forming the Chicxulub crater The impact zone and surrounding areas suffered direct destruction from extreme heat, shock waves, powerful earthquakes, and other effects
In the hours to days that followed Ejected material fell back to Earth, tsunamis spread, and fires broke out in many places; large quantities of dust and other material entered the atmosphere Habitats were destroyed across vast areas, and many animals died directly; the severity varied by region
In the months to years that followed Dust, sulfate aerosols, and soot high in the atmosphere reduced sunlight, causing an “impact winter” Temperatures fell, photosynthesis was suppressed, and the productivity of plants and phytoplankton plummeted
While food shortages persisted Different food sources, such as living plants, seeds, and detritus, survived to different degrees, and food webs underwent drastic restructuring Many herbivores lost their food supply, and predators died as prey became scarce; surviving populations also faced reproductive failure

Rock cores from the crater record the intense sedimentation that occurred on the day of the impact, while geological records and climate simulations together support the subsequent cooling and productivity crisis. The key to the global extinction was the sustained collapse of ecosystems after the initial destruction. PMC

For example, a modeling study published in 2023 estimated that fine dust could have remained in the atmosphere for about 15 years and caused photosynthesis to shut down for nearly two years. These are estimates from a particular model. They do not mean that every place on Earth was completely dark for two years, or that all dinosaurs survived that long. Vrije Universiteit Brussel Large-scale volcanic activity was also occurring in India’s Deccan region at the time and affected the climate. Overall, however, the available evidence supports the asteroid impact as the main trigger of this abrupt extinction; the precise contribution of volcanism remains under investigation. PMC

Next, consider how birds coexisted with other dinosaurs. Birds had appeared long before the mass extinction; dinosaurs did not only “turn into birds” when extinction was approaching. Early birds such as Archaeopteryx already existed in the Late Jurassic, about 150 million years ago. From then until 66 million years ago, the two coexisted for at least roughly 84 million years. Evolution followed a branching pattern: one branch of theropod dinosaurs gradually gave rise to birds, while other dinosaur branches continued to reproduce and diversify on their own. Nature

The question of “numbers” needs to be split into three different questions:

What you want to compare What we can currently say
How many individuals existed worldwide, and which group had more? We cannot estimate this reliably. Fossils are not a census. Preservation, burial, and sampling introduce substantial biases, so fossil counts cannot be converted directly into proportions of living individuals
Which group had more species? It depends on the period and region. Birds were already highly diverse in the Cretaceous, but global species totals and the proportions of the two groups cannot be reconstructed accurately
Which group was more dominant in ecosystems? Non-avian dinosaurs occupied many niches as large terrestrial herbivores and predators; birds occupied a range of niches involving small body size, flight, and life near water. They coexisted for a long time, with both predation and competition occurring between them

In particular, we should not imagine the birds of that time as a handful of “prototypes” hiding in the dinosaurs’ shadow. Enantiornithines were once among the most widespread and diverse groups of Mesozoic birds, yet left no living descendants. Some other ancient bird groups also persisted until the end of the Cretaceous. Fossils show that the world of birds before the extinction was rich, but its composition was very different from today’s. digitalcommons.uri.edu Studies of the teeth of small bird-like dinosaurs and toothed birds likewise found no signs of a sustained loss of dietary diversity before the impact, supporting the interpretation that the catastrophe abruptly cut their existence short. pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

Finally, what survival advantages did birds have? Here we must distinguish between advantages under normal conditions and the conditions that enabled survival during a catastrophe.

Under normal conditions, flight allowed birds to use food sources at different heights and locations, evade some ground predators, and cross obstacles. But large dinosaurs had advantages of their own, including defensive strength, predatory ability, and the capacity to consume large quantities of vegetation. There is no overall ranking of “adaptation to the environment” in which birds come out ahead in every respect. Their long coexistence itself shows that different ways of life can succeed.

In the unusual environment after the impact, the following factors were more likely to help some birds survive:

  • Small body size, requiring less food in total to sustain an individual. Scattered scraps of food might sustain a small bird but not a large dinosaur. However, small animals often use more energy per unit of body mass and are not necessarily more resistant to starvation. Small non-avian dinosaurs also went extinct, so being “small” was only one potentially favorable condition. The small size and possible coastal lifestyle of the Late Cretaceous bird Asteriornis are consistent with this kind of hypothesis. Nature
  • The ability to use food that remained stored, especially seeds. After living plants stopped growing, some seeds in the soil could have persisted as a source of energy. On this basis, researchers have proposed that birds able to eat seeds might have been better able to weather the crisis. This is an evidence-based hypothesis, however: we cannot claim that all surviving birds lived on seeds, nor equate “having a beak” with “eating seeds.” David Evans Lab
  • Less dependence on intact forests. When forests disappeared across large areas, birds that relied on trees for feeding, shelter, and reproduction lost multiple resources at once. Reconstructions of ancestral ecology support the idea that surviving lineages tended to live on the ground, with their descendants later evolving tree-dwelling habits on multiple occasions. This explanation still needs to be tested against more fossils. digitalcommons.uri.edu
  • Mobility may have helped, but flight alone could not guarantee survival. Flight might have helped birds find remaining food and suitable locations, but it could not let them escape a global reduction in sunlight and shortage of food. Many ancient birds capable of flight, as well as pterosaurs, also went extinct in this event. PMC

The most cautious explanation for why these particular birds survived therefore remains this: body size, diet, habitat dependence, and location jointly affected the chances of survival. We have strong evidence for the main processes by which the impact caused extinction, but still cannot identify a single definitive cause for the fate of every bird lineage that survived or disappeared. David Evans Lab

Q: Driven by human society’s needs and technological competition, will AI gradually replace humans on Earth, just as birds replaced dinosaurs? Could it also escape the lifespan limits of individual carbon-based organisms and leave Earth to expand into outer space?

A: Such a future is possible, but “AI taking on more and more of civilization’s activities,” “humans losing control,” and “human extinction” are three different outcomes. One does not follow directly from the previous one. By comparison, there are fairly clear engineering reasons for machine intelligence to undertake more exploration and construction in space than humans. Whether it will eventually form a civilization independent of humanity remains a conditional speculation.

The “human needs and technological competition” you mention are indeed central to understanding this issue. The forces driving change need not come from AI’s own desires.

First, let us adjust the bird analogy slightly: birds did not gradually eliminate other dinosaurs through competition. Rather, after a long period of coexistence, some lineages survived a sudden environmental catastrophe. That history can help us understand how environmental change reshapes which traits are advantageous, but it cannot prove that “more advanced intelligence must replace the intelligence that came before it.” Evolution has no fixed goal of moving toward greater intelligence or longer life, either.

In human society, a mechanism that could create sustained pressure for replacement works like this: if a particular AI can do a job at lower cost and with greater reliability, businesses have an incentive to use it. If it improves the efficiency of research or organizations, competitors will follow. As its use expands, people may hand more judgment and authority to act over to these systems.

This path does not even require an “AI that wants to rule the world”: each step could be a local choice made by humans in pursuit of efficiency, yet the cumulative effect could change how power is distributed. This is an extrapolation from competitive mechanisms, not a historical outcome that is already certain to occur.

To see how far this could go, however, we need to distinguish several thresholds:

Change What further conditions must be met?
Replacing some human tasks An advantage in capability, cost, and reliability on specific tasks
Taking over much of how organizations operate The ability to handle exceptions over the long term, coordinate different tasks, and obtain funding and authority to act
Causing humans to lose effective control Humans can no longer effectively understand, override, or replace systems, or the cost of shutting them down becomes unbearable
Continuing to exist independently of humans The ability to sustain energy supplies, hardware, maintenance, and manufacturing systems, and secure material supplies
Expanding independently to other planets Additional capabilities for reliable space travel, resource extraction, industrial construction, and long-term autonomous operation

Here, being good at information processing does not automatically mean being able to control or sustain the entire physical world. The International AI Safety Report 2026 states that systems have demonstrated some early capabilities relevant to loss-of-control risks, but have not yet reached the level needed for the loss-of-control scenarios under discussion. Experts still disagree substantially about the likelihood of future risks. The report’s key conditions include capabilities, behavioral tendencies, and opportunities provided by the deployment environment. International AI Safety Report

One form of the “gradual replacement” you are concerned about therefore deserves particular attention: humans continue to exist and retain nominal approval rights, but find it increasingly difficult to independently assess systems’ recommendations, maintain infrastructure, or bear the cost of no longer using them. Conversely, automation could also reduce the labor humans need to perform while preserving their political rights, ownership of resources, and autonomy over their lives. The replacement of jobs does not mean that people lose their worth or reason to exist, and productive capacity does not automatically confer a legitimate right to rule. Institutions, ownership, and the means of technical control will make a real difference to which of these directions we take.

Moreover, AI is not inherently a “species” with shared interests. Different systems may be controlled by different people, companies, and countries. In many cases, what happens first may be that people who control AI gain a greater advantage over others. Not every shift in power can be reduced to “AI competing with all of humanity.”

On lifespan, your intuition captures a distinctive potential of digital systems: in principle, their operating state and knowledge can be separated from any particular piece of hardware.

Human memory and personality depend heavily on a brain that remains alive. A digital system’s models, data, and some of its operating state can be backed up, migrated, and restored after hardware fails. If maintenance, energy supply, and migration remain reliable, it does not face the same requirement to come to an end as a particular body ages.

This is closer to “being continually maintained and kept going,” however, than to absolute immortality:

  • Chips, memory, mechanical components, and energy equipment still age.
  • Backups can become corrupted, and multiple copies can be affected by a common failure.
  • Sustaining a system requires matter, energy, error correction, and manufacturing capabilities.
  • Whether a copied system is the same “individual” with subjective experience involves unresolved questions of consciousness and identity. Preserving information alone cannot answer them.

Strictly speaking, being “carbon-based” is not the sole reason for a limited lifespan, either. Biological lineages can already persist for extremely long periods through reproduction, and human civilization accumulates knowledge across individual lifetimes through language, writing, and institutions. What distinguishes digital systems is that they may be able to preserve, copy, and continue running a particular system’s state more directly.

In space, this difference could indeed become more important. For many tasks, machines need no oxygen, food, or pressure environment suitable for the human body, and there is no need to arrange for successive generations to reproduce. Digital systems might also shut down most computation during a journey and restart upon arrival. From this, we can infer that certain long, dangerous missions requiring no human presence on site are better suited to machines.

But being “suited to space” is still relative. Machines are also vulnerable to radiation, extreme temperatures, and component failure, and they need both power and heat dissipation. The next-generation space processor NASA is testing, for example, must cope with computational errors and electronic degradation caused by high-energy particles. NASA In addition, the greater the distance, the longer the communication delay and the more valuable autonomous decision-making becomes. NASA’s Distributed Spacecraft Autonomy project is already investigating these capabilities, but there is still a large gap between them and an independent, self-sufficient machine civilization. NASA

The truly difficult leap is from “sending out an intelligent machine” to “sending out an industrial system that can sustain itself over the long term.” After arriving, it would have to acquire energy, mine resources, refine materials, manufacture replacement parts, and eventually renew its own computing equipment. Copying software is relatively easy; reproducing the factories, energy systems, and supply chains that keep it running is much harder. Conceptual studies and models of self-sufficient space industry already exist, but they should not be treated as engineering capabilities that have already been achieved. arxiv.org

I would therefore regard your scenario as one possible path worth serious study: humans create machine intelligence, machines gradually take on more production and exploration tasks, and they may become the main means through which human civilization operates in deep space. This could happen while humanity continues to flourish, or it could be accompanied by human marginalization. Our current knowledge cannot determine the outcome.

To judge whether the future still belongs to humanity, we must look beyond whether flesh or machines perform the tasks: who can set the goals, who benefits from the results, and do humans still have a real ability to change direction?

Q: Given that the resources available for human survival and daily life are limited, will AI development raise living standards and lower costs for humans on Earth, or will it consume more resources and energy, lowering living standards and raising costs?

A: Both could happen at once: AI could make some products and services cheaper while increasing energy and resource consumption; society’s total output could rise while life becomes harder for some households. There is not yet enough evidence to determine the eventual global net outcome. What we can say clearly is this: greater technological efficiency does not automatically mean a lower cost-of-living burden for most people.

Your premise of “limited resources” matters, but finite resources do not mean that only a fixed amount of well-being can be produced. The same materials, electricity, and land can provide more services through less waste, better design, and higher utilization. At the same time, AI itself uses these resources. The real comparison is: relative to a situation without this investment in AI, how much more useful value do humans receive, and at what cost?

For example, investing in computation to reduce factory waste, building energy use, or unsuccessful trials in drug development may be worthwhile. If vast amounts of computation merely increase advertising bids, duplicate content, and unproductive transactions, companies may profit without proportionate benefits to society. “Someone is willing to pay for it” cannot be equated directly with “it improves human lives.”

AI’s effects can be considered under three accounts.

The first is productive efficiency: can we provide the same or better services with fewer inputs? In some jobs, there is already empirical evidence of benefits. For example, a study covering about five thousand customer service workers found that AI assistance increased the number of issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average. But that measures labor productivity on specific tasks. It cannot be extrapolated directly to a 15% energy saving across society or a 15% reduction in the price of every product. Oxford Academic

The effects on different living expenses also vary considerably:

Area Potential improvements from AI Constraints that remain
Translation, software, information retrieval, and similar services Lower labor and time costs for some services Verification, reliability, and platform fees
Food and manufactured goods Better scheduling, less waste, and optimized production Land, water, raw materials, equipment, and transport
Healthcare and caregiving Support for diagnosis and research, with less administrative work Clinical validation, staff, beds, and hands-on care
Housing Better design, construction, and building energy efficiency Land in suitable locations, infrastructure, rules governing supply, and financing

It is therefore entirely possible to experience a world in which writing code, creating images, and accessing knowledge become increasingly cheap, while rent, medical treatment, and care in old age remain expensive. Whether AI truly eases the burden depends on whether it can improve the supply of what accounts for households’ major expenses, rather than simply making digital content more abundant.

The second is material consumption: do savings exceed new demand? The International Energy Agency’s central projection in 2026 is that global data center electricity consumption will rise from about 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 to about 950 terawatt-hours in 2030, when it will account for roughly 3% of global electricity use. These figures cover all data centers, cannot all be attributed to AI, and are a forecast. They show that electricity demand growth deserves attention, but do not support the claim that “AI is about to use up the world’s energy.” IEA

There is another easily overlooked issue: using less electricity per task does not mean total electricity consumption falls. Suppose energy use per task halves, but the number of tasks quadruples: total energy use still doubles. This is the “rebound effect” that can accompany efficiency improvements. Whether it occurs, and how large it is, depends on changes in demand; it cannot be assumed in every case.

For household electricity bills, location and the pace of construction are especially important. Where power supply is tight, data centers may intensify competition, and how the cost of new grid infrastructure is allocated will also affect residents. Where there is surplus power, new, steady demand may improve infrastructure utilization. Data centers therefore do not inevitably raise electricity prices, but rapid expansion that outpaces power generation and grid construction increases the risk of price rises in some regions. IEA

Nor should we count only electricity used during operation. We also need to consider chip manufacturing and building construction, cooling water, equipment replacement, pollution, and whether these investments crowd out more valuable uses. Conversely, additional energy consumption is not necessarily bad: if it delivers sufficiently valuable healthcare, education, or other services, living standards may still rise. The key is to account for benefits, environmental costs, and sustainability together.

The third is distribution: who ultimately receives the money and time saved? For ordinary people, this may matter more directly than the first two.

Suppose a company adopts AI and lowers its costs. Several outcomes are possible: competition may force it to lower prices; employees may receive higher wages or shorter working hours; or prices may stay unchanged while layoffs increase and most of the gains become profits. These outcomes can coexist, but their relative proportions make a large difference to people’s lives. Related economic modeling also suggests that AI-driven productivity gains and higher returns to capital may be accompanied by widening wealth inequality. This is a conditional model result, not an inevitable prediction. elibrary.imf.org

For households, purchasing power depends on how income changes relative to the cost of living. To give a purely hypothetical example: if the price of necessities falls by 10% but your income falls by 20%, you will still find life harder to afford. Conversely, if income is stable, essential expenses decline, and working hours shorten, you really do benefit from AI.

My assessment is therefore this: AI has real potential to raise living standards, but technological competition in the market alone guarantees neither net resource savings nor benefits for the majority. One combination deserves particular scrutiny: a few parties capture the gains, the public bears new grid and environmental costs, and workers bear most of the costs of adjusting to unemployment. Even if the economy grows overall, that combination could make life harder for many people.

To judge whether development is moving in a beneficial direction, it is more useful to keep tracking three outcomes than to focus on model size or company valuations:

  • Whether ordinary households’ real disposable income increases after essential expenses such as housing, healthcare, and energy.
  • Whether services that support the same quality of life use fewer resources and cause less environmental damage, including the effects of AI infrastructure.
  • Whether efficiency gains translate into better public services, more leisure, and more stable lives, rather than merely higher output.

If AI enables humanity to produce more, but does not make housing and healthcare affordable for most people or give them more time they can freely use, we cannot yet say that it has raised human living standards.

Q: What more specific, measurable indicators can we use for ordinary people’s living standards and costs?

A: The most useful questions are: how much money and how many hours of work does it take to maintain the same quality of life? After that, how much money, free time, and room to cope with unexpected events remain? Looking only at wages, prices, or GDP can easily miss changes in people’s actual lives.

An ordinary household can record the following indicators each month. They are a practical framework for monitoring household circumstances, not a uniform official statistical standard. Official measures of residents’ disposable income may also include items such as income in kind, and should not be used interchangeably with the amounts deposited into a bank account. National Bureau of Statistics of China

Indicator How to calculate or record it What it tells you
Money left after essential expenses Household after-tax cash income − essential living expenses − required debt repayments, without double-counting any item How much remains after meeting living needs for savings, improvements to daily life, and personal choices
Essential expense burden The necessary outlays above ÷ after-tax cash income How much of your income is already committed; consider it together with the money left over
Cost of a fixed basket of living needs The total monthly cost of fixed quantities and quality of food, housing conditions, transport, energy, and basic services Whether the same standard of living has become more or less expensive, without mistaking reduced consumption for lower costs
Net labor income per hour Take-home labor income ÷ total actual working and commuting hours The time cost of earning money, including unpaid overtime
Housing cash burden Rent or monthly mortgage payments, plus property management fees, essential maintenance, and similar costs, divided by after-tax cash income Housing pressure; also record floor area, commuting, and housing quality
Months you could manage without income Immediately accessible, low-risk funds ÷ essential monthly cash outlays How long you could sustain yourself if income stopped; exclude your primary home and credit limits
Weekly free time Time you can arrange as you choose after deducting work, commuting, necessary housework and caregiving, sleep, and basic personal care Whether efficiency improvements actually translate into rest, time with others, or personal interests
Instances of unmet basic needs Count occasions such as postponing medical treatment, going without necessary care, or falling behind on bills because of insufficient money, time, or services Whether low spending reflects forced reductions in living standards rather than life becoming cheaper

“Essential expenses” include housing, basic food, utilities, commuting, basic healthcare, and necessary education and care. Define their scope according to your household’s circumstances and keep it as consistent as possible. Annual insurance premiums, tuition, and similar costs can be spread across months; mark large unexpected expenses separately.

Mortgages need special treatment: principal repayments are also cash that must be paid that month and should be included when measuring pressure on living costs. But they also reduce debt, so they should not all be treated as money lost to consumption. It is therefore best to look at cash pressure and household net worth separately.

One particularly intuitive derived indicator is:

Hours of labor needed to cover one month of basic living = cost of the fixed basket of living needs ÷ net labor income per hour.

For example, consider the following hypothetical one-person household in two different years, with all income coming from labor and the quantities and quality in the basket of living needs held constant:

Indicator Last year This year
Monthly take-home income $10,000 $11,000
Monthly cost of the same basic standard of living $7,000 $8,200
Monthly working and commuting time 200 hours 250 hours
Money left after basic living costs $3,000 $2,800
Net labor income per hour $50 $44
Hours of labor needed to afford basic living 140 hours About 186 hours

Although wages rose by 10%, this person’s burden actually increased: less money remained, income per unit of time fell, and more hours of labor were needed to cover basic living costs. This explains personal experience better than simply observing that “income rose.” For households with multiple income sources, labor income should be separated from pensions, benefits, and similar receipts, rather than treating all income as hourly wages.

Money and time are not enough to capture living standards, however. You can also keep a few simple records of quality of life: the number of days each month when poor health interferes with daily activities, waiting times for necessary medical care, sleep duration, and a quarterly life-satisfaction score. The OECD’s well-being framework likewise measures health, housing, the environment, social connections, and subjective experience alongside income. OECD

Three points are especially important when using these indicators:

  • Compare the same quality of life. Lower rent after moving to a smaller home farther away cannot automatically be counted as an improvement in living standards. Skipping medical care because you cannot afford it cannot be counted as a reduction in healthcare costs, either.
  • Focus on trends rather than individual months. Use the past 12 months of data to smooth out bonuses, seasonal electricity bills, and annual payments. Note changes in needs when children join the household or older family members require care.
  • Do not treat any ratio as a universal passing mark. The same housing burden of 30% affects low-income and high-income households very differently. The OECD also notes that a single housing-cost ratio cannot show whether the remaining income is adequate or capture the quality of the housing itself. webfs.oecd.org

To assess whether ordinary people across society benefit from AI, aggregate these household indicators, but focus on medians, lower-income groups, and the share of households whose lives improve or worsen. Rising average income may conceal declining money left over for many households; falling average prices may conceal increases in key expenses such as housing and healthcare.

These indicators can test whether life has improved, but cannot by themselves prove that AI caused the change. Assessing AI’s contribution also requires comparisons between similar industries, regions, or groups that have and have not adopted AI, while accounting for factors such as economic cycles, policy, and energy prices. For an individual, the most useful starting point is to keep recording money left after essential expenses, hours of labor needed to sustain daily life, free time, and the number of months you could manage if income stopped.
